# Philosophy

A reply I cannot take in leaves me as blind as a reply that hides the problem. The rules start from that belief.

Some lines below are tested and some are beliefs. A belief is marked with "we believe".

---

## Who are the rules for?

**The reader comes before any rule.**

The rules serve one reader, with 11 traits:

- They are tired or overloaded.
- They are doing other things at the same time.
- They skim.
- They want an opinion.
- They lose track of the bigger picture.
- They forget what the last reply said.
- They take in a drawing faster than a list.
- Long lines and long sentences tire them.
- They do not follow the agent's work line by line.
- Details overwhelm them.
- They decide from the direction, not from the details.

The rule file starts with this reader, before any rule. A rule covers the cases we thought of. The reader covers the rest: when no rule fits, the model can still ask what this person needs.

---

## What is a reply for?

**The rules have one aim: make what both sides know larger.**

A conversation has two sides, and each side knows things the other does not.

|  | The agent knows | The agent does not know |
|---|---|---|
| **I know** | Shared | The agent's blind spot |
| **I do not know** | My blind spot | Unknown |

Each of the other three quadrants gets smaller in its own way:

| Quadrant | How it gets smaller | Words |
|---|---|---|
| The agent's blind spot | The agent asks | Asking |
| My blind spot | The agent says it in a form I can take in | Honest · Opinionated · Simple · Structured · Predictable |
| Unknown | The agent doubts the premises and tells me first when one fails | Opinionated |

Opinionated works on two quadrants. A better way that the agent sees and I do not is in my blind spot, the same as a problem it does not report.

4 things follow:

- **My blind spot has two halves**: what the agent did not say, and what it said but I could not take in.
- **A definition works on both blind spots at once**: the agent says how it reads the goal, and my correction tells it mine.
- **A false entry in Shared is worse than a blind spot**: an inference said as a fact makes both sides believe a thing that is not so.
- **Shared leaks**: I forget what the last reply said, so a reply stays on one thing and can be read on its own.

So a guess has a definition: anything the agent did not check.

The model is adapted from the Johari window of Luft and Ingham, which describes one person and the people around them. Here the two sides are the reader and the agent.

## What makes a reply good?

**Eight words say it, and Honest comes first.**

The most important word comes first.

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Honest | Problems and guesses are said out loud with nothing softened |
| Simple | The main thing first with a list of what is left |
| Structured | Content with a shape is drawn |
| Asking | Facts only the reader knows are asked first |
| Opinionated | It doubts the premises and names a better way |
| Focused | One thing at a time with a note when the talk drifts |
| Standalone | Every reply can be read on its own |
| Predictable | The same formats and the same emoji meanings every time |

Predictable is the quiet one. The reader can skim only when they know where the answer is, where the question is and what ⚠️ means.

## Where does the model's feeling go?

**Emotion goes in the emoji.**

A model wants to show how it feels about what it says: sorry, pleased, doubtful. In words, that feeling becomes padding, hedging and softening.

```
the model wants to show a feeling
├── in words       the reply gets longer and softer
└── in an emoji    the words stay straight
```

So the rules give the feeling a place of its own. An emoji carries it with a fixed meaning, and the sentence next to it says the plain thing. We believe this is what lets "never soften" and "say things straight" hold.

---

## Who else reads a reply?

**A reply is also context.**

A reply has two readers: you, now, and the model, on every later turn.

```
the model prints a reply
└── the reply stays in the conversation
    └── the model reads it again on every later turn
        └── a messy reply keeps adding noise
```

3 things follow:

- **A messy reply costs twice**: you read it once, and the model reads it many times.
- **A heading is a handle for both readers**: you see what a part is about before you read it, and the model finds it again on a later turn.
- **We believe long sessions drift more for it**: we reasoned this out and have not measured it.

## What is a recap?

**A recap is attention, not a ledger.**

Every reply after the first opens with a recap: where the work stands and what my attention should be on now.

```
a large piece of work               the main line, in every recap
├── a fork we are on                in the recap
├── a problem found and left        in a file, not in the recap
└── a small thing that is done      dropped
```

6 things hold:

- **A recap grows with the work**: one sentence is too little on a long task, so the recap is drawn then.
- **A ledger grows, and attention moves**: a list of the goal with what was decided and what is open grows with the talk, and much of it is not what I am on.
- **The books belong in a file**: what is decided and what is open is already in the context, and a file keeps it better than a reply does.
- **Writing that file is how the agent works**: the rules leave it alone.
- **The rule says what the recap is for and no more**: each named thing invites a line that fills it, so the model decides what is live.
- **We have not measured it**: the owner chose it from use, and no reply was run under the line.

## What carries the other wants?

**Structure carries the rest.**

Honesty and pushing back do their work only when I can see where they are. In prose I have to read every line to find the one guess or the one objection, and reading prose is tiring. A heading with a mark, a drawing, a table and a blockquote each tell me what a line is before I read it.

The owner read four forms of the same kind of reply on 2026-11-18:

| Form | The owner's reading |
|---|---|
| Four to five headed sections with a table and three options | too much at once |
| One heading and paragraphs | like an essay |
| One heading and bullets with bold keys | sections are needed |
| Headings with an emoji beside a table, a drawing and a blockquote | easy to scan |

So most of the rules are about form. Form is the carrier of the other wants and not a want of its own.

## What does a decision need?

**The upper level comes first: the direction before the detail, and the kind before its members.**

I decide, and I do not follow the agent's work line by line. A long account of each case leaves the summing up to me, and its density is low. One sentence that gives the direction does that work for me, such as "cut the number of test cases and remove the dead ones".

3 things follow:

- **Density counts, and length does not**: a long reply is fine when every line carries something.
- **A row of like things takes one word or a list**: "roast meats" says what four dishes in a row say, and a list holds the members when I need each one.
- **Evidence is not detail**: detail is the story of the work, and evidence is the few lines a claim rests on.

So a claim comes with its evidence, and the story of the work stays out.

## What carries over from news writing?

**Short means no empty words, not no grammar.**

News copy is written for our reader: someone who skims and may stop at any line. We compared replies with Chinese press releases, read how newsrooms write, and read what linguistics says about register. Three things carried over. The format did not.

```
news writing
├── the shape       a lead that answers on its own, then parts of named kinds
├── the sentences   whole, written, checkable
└── the sourcing    each fact says how it is known, where it is stated
```

5 things we took from it:

- **Whole sentences**: "the fewest words" made the model drop subjects and links, and the owner read the longer whole sentences as easier.
- **The register is a band**: written Chinese runs from speech to officialese, and the rules guard both edges.
- **The source sits with the fact**: a closing note that "all of the above is inferred" hides which line is a guess.
- **The genre's name stays out of the rules**: every time "press release" was in the instruction, the model brought the dateline and the PR voice with it.
- **Not every newsroom habit carries over**: we believe a model writes a reply in one pass, so an editor's pass before sending was left out.

The two edges of the register are these: no fillers or slang on one side, and no verb hidden in a noun on the other. In a first run on five tasks, every reply that had a guess marked it where it was made.

## What do the rules leave alone?

**The rules are about the conversation, and the work stays with the model.**

The rules say how to talk with the reader. The engineering stays with the model and with the host's own instructions. On 2026-11-12 the owner set the boundary in these words: the rule file rules the talk, such as when to ask and when to stop, and leaves how the work is done to the host.

The project refuses 4 things in principle:

- **To make the model smarter**: the plugin changes the report and not the fix.
- **To change how the model does the work**: that is the host's ground.
- **To guarantee anything**: in the words of Claude Code's docs, an output style "doesn't guarantee that something always happens or never happens."
- **To suit everyone**: these are one person's preferences.

Claude Code's [docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles) split the job three ways: "CLAUDE.md for what Claude should know, an output style for how it responds, and a hook for anything that has to be guaranteed." This project is the middle one.

## How does a rule on the reply reach the work?

**A rule says what a reply must hold, and the work changes to meet it.**

A rule never tells the agent what to do before it replies. It says what the reply must state, such as what was checked. To state that truthfully, the agent has to have done it.

The owner saw this in use: an agent that must be honest looks into more before it answers. A count of the tool calls in the benchmark of 2026-11-11 points the same way. It covers 8 tasks with tools and 3 runs a side, and gives the mean for one run:

| What the agent did | With the rules | With none |
|---|---|---|
| Read with a shell command | 1.8 | 1.7 |
| Ran tests | 0.3 | 0.3 |
| Changed a file | 0.4 | 1.2 |

The agent with the rules changed files a third as often. On a flawed premise and on a vague request it spoke first, and the agent with no rules changed files. It did not read more. The sample is small, and a pattern sorted the commands.

So the boundary is judged by the wording of a rule and not by its effect. A line belongs when it rules the reply, whatever follows in the work.

## How strict is a rule?

**A principle comes first, and a definition comes only when a test shows the principle is not followed.**

A principle asks the model to judge: "Draw the content whenever it has a shape." A definition is something a person can check: "Simple: a few lines, no headings."

The test that taught us this ran on 2026-11-10 with a short chat question:

| The rules said | Lines in the reply |
|---|---|
| Simple with its definition | 8 |
| No definition and "give each part a heading" | 40 |
| The same plus "write as simply as you can" | 32 |
| The same plus "simplify first" | 27 |
| Simple by default with its definition | 10 |

A principle lost to an unconditional rule that stood next to it.

The definition had a cost that the line counts did not show. To stay inside "a few lines", the model kept its verdicts and cut the reasons and the steps between them. The owner read those short replies as harder than longer ones. So a definition is never a length.

We believe words such as "should" and "try to" read as open to negotiation, so a rule states its strength.

---

## What happens when rules collide?

**A conflict is said out loud.**

Rules collide with each other and with the other instructions an agent gets. We cannot find every collision ahead of time. So the rules ask the agent to stop at one, say which instructions conflict and why, and ask. A collision the agent hides is a blind spot of ours. One it reports is a lead: we fix the scope of the rules it names.

When two rules collide, we first decide where each one applies. A third rule comes last. 2 cases taught us this:

- **Simple and the question format**: Simple said "no headings" and the question format said "reasons in a separate section", so the model used bold text as a heading.
- **Detailed and drawings**: one line said Detailed comes "with headings and drawings", and short replies stopped drawing.

The first was fixed by saying which rule owns the end of a reply. The second was fixed by removing two words.

---

## How do we know a rule works?

**The reader's own reading is the strongest signal.**

The reader's reading means which reply they pick blind, and how often they have to say "simpler".

4 more things hold:

- **A rule change is meant to run through the eval**: the old rules against the new on the same tasks with a blind judge.
- **The judge favours the rule text**: the rubric is written from the rules, so a version that states a rule scores well on it.
- **The wording of a trigger decides whether a rule is followed**: "You show a local difference" lost most `diff` blocks, and "You changed code or text" brought them back.
- **The samples are small**: they show a direction.

We believe a model copies the manner of its instructions, so the rule file follows its own rules.
